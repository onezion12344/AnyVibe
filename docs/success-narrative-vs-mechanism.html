Success Narratives vs Success Mechanisms

What folk psychology claims about entrepreneurial success, what science actually shows, and how to tell an honest story without the survivorship bias theater

1. Folk Psychology Audit

FOLK PSYCHOLOGY

自我标榜 (Self-Branding)

No validated psychological construct exists for "self-branding." The underlying behaviors map to signaling theory (Spence, 1973), impression management (Goffman, 1959), and narcissism (NPI, Raskin & Terry, 1988).

Key insight: Verbal self-promotion is cheap talk. Costly signals — GitHub commits, user growth data, third-party endorsements — are what rational observers should weigh. Most "personal branding" in startup culture is identity performance without verification cost.

Cheap Talk Signaling Theory NPI
MIXED — PHILOSOPHY ≠ PSYCHOLOGY

主体性 (Agency / Subjectivity)

Two distinct meanings are conflated: philosophical subjectivity (Descartes, Husserl — first-person perspective, unmeasurable) and psychological agency (Bandura's self-efficacy, Rotter's locus of control — validated constructs).

Key insight: When someone says "you need more 主体性," they could mean: be proactive (behavioral activation), believe in yourself (self-efficacy, r=.38 with performance per Stajkovic & Luthans meta-analysis), take responsibility (internal locus of control), or make your own judgments (autonomy per SDT). Vague = unfalsifiable = cheap.

Self-Efficacy Locus of Control SDT Autonomy
FOLK NARRATIVE — ZERO EMPIRICAL EVIDENCE

初心 (Beginner's Mind / Original Intention)

Shoshin is a Zen spiritual concept (Suzuki, 1970), not a scientific construct. In modern Chinese, "不忘初心" is a political slogan (Xi Jinping, 2016) later absorbed into startup rhetoric. No empirical research validates "初心" as a predictor of anything.

Key insight: Every successful founder claims they kept their 初心. So did 90% of those who failed. The concept is circular: success proves初心, failure proves its absence. Closest scientific constructs: cognitive flexibility (CFS, Dennis & Vander Wal, 2010) and intellectual humility (Leary et al., 2017).

2. Why Vague Concepts Dominate Startup Culture

Fuzziness = Unfalsifiability = Safety

"You need more 初心" can never be proven wrong. There is no operational definition, no measurement, no counterfactual test. This makes it a perfect narrative device — it explains everything and risks nothing.

Identity Signaling (Bourdieu's Cultural Capital)

Using these words signals you belong to the "startup tribe." It's a shibboleth — not designed to convey information, but to mark insider status. The vaguer the concept, the stronger the identity signal.

Post-Hoc Rationalization (Taleb's Narrative Fallacy)

Success demands a story. "I made 100 correct micro-decisions and got lucky on timing" is not a good story. "I kept my 初心 and had strong 主体性" is. The story replaces the mechanism because the story is what people want to hear — and what the successful person wants to believe about themselves.

Control Illusion

"If I maintain 初心 / strengthen 主体性, I will succeed" implies success is controllable through internal states — ignoring the 90% startup base failure rate, market timing, and structural factors. This is psychologically comforting but statistically false.

Confidence Filter — Who Gets to Speak

Only the highly confident (sometimes pathologically so) give talks, write threads, and dominate hackathon stages. Those who are cautious, uncertain, and nuanced self-select out of visibility. What we hear is the most confident 5%, not the most accurate 5%.

2.5. The Narrative—Mechanism Gap — Attribution Theory

8,180 CITATIONS

Motivated Reasoning (Kunda, 1990)

People arrive at desired conclusions by selectively recruiting evidence — not blind wishing, but a sophisticated process of constructing seemingly reasonable justifications. Founders genuinely construct causal narratives where their agency is central. The 初心 story feels true because it has been processed through a motivated reasoning filter that discards counterevidence.

Kunda, Z. (1990). The case for motivated reasoning. Psychological Bulletin, 108(3), 480–498.
FOUNDATIONAL

Self-Serving Bias — It's Perceptual, Not Strategic (Miller & Ross, 1975)

People systematically take personal credit for success and externalize failure. This is NOT mere self-promotion — it is cognitive. People genuinely perceive stronger covariation between their actions and successful outcomes than exists. The person on stage is not lying. The distortion is perceptual.

Miller, D.T. & Ross, M. (1975). Self-serving biases in the attribution of causality. Psychological Bulletin, 82(2), 213–225.
DIRECT ENTREPRENEURIAL EVIDENCE

Actor-Observer Bias in Founders (Rogoff, Lee & Suh, 2004)

Entrepreneurs attribute success to internal factors (effort, skills, decisions). Experts studying the same businesses see more external causation (market timing, luck). The person doing the thing sees their own agency. The observer sees the environment. Both are biased — in opposite directions.

Rogoff, E.G., Lee, M.S. & Suh, D.C. (2004). Who done it? Journal of Small Business Management, 42(4), 364–376.

2.6. Narrative Psychology — Stories as Sense-Making, Not Truth

FOUNDATIONAL

Two Modes of Thought (Bruner, 1986/1991)

Paradigmatic mode: logical-scientific — truth, verification, cause-effect. Narrative mode: meaning, believability, coherence, human agency. A good story is not a true story — it is a coherent story. Startup pitches operate in narrative mode. The test is "does this inspire?" — not "does this isolate actual causal factors?"

Bruner, J. (1986). Actual Minds, Possible Worlds. (1991). The narrative construction of reality. Critical Inquiry, 18(1), 1–21.
STRONG EVIDENCE

Life Stories Are Reconstructive (McAdams, 2001)

People construct internalized, evolving narratives to provide unity and purpose — shaped by current concerns and cultural scripts, not accurate historical records. 初心 and 主体性 are culturally scripted identity themes, not causal variables. The self that tells the story is not the self that experienced the events.

McAdams, D.P. (2001). The psychology of life stories. Review of General Psychology, 5(2), 100–122.
STRONG EVIDENCE

Entrepreneurial Storytelling Paradox (Garud, Schildt & Lant, 2014)

Entrepreneurs use projective stories to win legitimacy. The expectations that win legitimacy become the source of future disappointments. Storytellers edit out dead ends, imbue random pivots with strategic genius, and create coherence that never existed in real time. The narrative is systematically different from lived experience.

Garud, R., Schildt, H. & Lant, T.K. (2014). Entrepreneurial storytelling and the paradox of legitimacy. Organization Science, 25(5), 1479–1497.
EMPIRICAL · 3,220 VERIFIED RECORDS

Memory Itself Inflates (Bahrick, Hall & Berger, 1996)

89% accuracy recalling A's, 29% for D's. Most errors inflated the grade. Autobiographical memory has a built-in upward distortion function. Founders narrate from already-distorted memories — distortion compounds on distortion.

Bahrick, H.P., Hall, L.K. & Berger, S.A. (1996). Accuracy and distortion in memory. Psychological Science, 7(5), 265–269.

2.7. Hindsight Bias — "I Knew It All Along"

FOUNDATIONAL

The Original (Fischhoff, 1975)

Receiving outcome knowledge increases postdicted likelihood regardless of actual likelihood. Judges were unaware of this effect. After a startup succeeds, founders genuinely believe their early decisions were more calculated than they were. The "knew-it-all-along" effect rewires memory.

Fischhoff, B. (1975). Hindsight is not equal to foresight. J. Experimental Psychology: HPP, 1(3), 288–299.
COMPREHENSIVE REVIEW

Three Components (Roese & Vohs, 2012)

Hindsight bias = memory distortion ("I said it would happen") + inevitability ("it had to happen") + foreseeability ("I knew it would happen"). Startup narratives display all three. "I always believed in this vision" is not accurate recall — it is hindsight bias.

Roese, N.J. & Vohs, K.D. (2012). Hindsight bias. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 7(5), 411–426.
THE EVIDENCE

3. What Psychology Shows — The Evidence

STRONG EVIDENCE · NBER / Journal of Finance

Persuasive Delivery Predicts Funding — Negatively Predicts Performance

Machine learning analysis of full pitch videos found: warm, passionate pitches increase funding probability. BUT conditional on getting funded, high-passion startups underperform. The mechanism: persuasive delivery creates inaccurate investor beliefs.

Hu, A. & Ma, S. (2021). Persuading Investors: A Video-Based Study. NBER Working Paper No. 29048, published in Journal of Finance.
Critical Finding
STRONG EVIDENCE · fMRI Study

Founder Passion Increases Neural Engagement by 39%

High-passion founders trigger significantly higher neural engagement in investors, mediating a 26% boost in investment interest. But this is a persuasion effect — not a performance signal.

Shane, S., Drover, W., Clingingsmith, D., & Cerf, M. (2020). Founder passion, neural engagement, and informal investor interest. Journal of Business Venturing, 35(4).
STRONG EVIDENCE · Pre-Registered Experiment

Success Stories Cause False Beliefs — Even When Bias Is Acknowledged

Participants shown dropout-founder success stories became 55 percentage points more likely to bet on dropout-founded companies. Acknowledging the bias did not prevent the distortion. Most reported medium-to-high confidence and generated causal explanations to justify their decisions.

Lifchits, G., Anderson, A., Goldstein, D.G., Hofman, J.M., & Watts, D.J. (2021). Success stories cause false beliefs about success. Judgment and Decision Making, 16(6), 1439–1463.
Critical Finding
STRONG EVIDENCE · Longitudinal Data

Entrepreneurs Recalibrate Past Success Probabilities by ~20pp

Using PSED longitudinal data on 198 nascent entrepreneurs: those who abandoned their ventures showed substantial hindsight bias, recalibrating recalled success probabilities from 77.3% down to 58.8%. Only those with the most formal education were immune.

Cassar, G. & Craig, J. (2009). An investigation of hindsight bias in nascent venture activity. Journal of Business Venturing, 24(2), 149–164.
STRONG EVIDENCE · MIT PhD

Narrative Backlash — Investors Often Reject Stories, Demand Data

Only 33% of Shark Tank entrepreneurs told a story in their pitch. Even when investors acknowledged stories as high-quality, coherent, and resonant, they often dismissed them in favor of facts and substance. The paper introduces "narrative rebound" as a documented phenomenon.

Turner, B. (2023). The storytelling entrepreneur has no clothes: risks and rewards of narrative pitching. MIT PhD Dissertation.
Counter-Narrative
STRONG EVIDENCE · Meta-Analysis (50 effect sizes, N=15,751)

Time Pressure Has a J-Curve Effect on Innovation

High time pressure is more positively associated with innovation performance than moderate pressure. Learning behavior statistically mediates the relationship. Supervisor developmental feedback moderates it.

Song, H., Zhang, Q., & Li, Y. (2023). Nonlinear effects of time pressure on innovation performance. Frontiers in Psychology, 13.
Hackathon-Relevant
STRONG EVIDENCE · Academy of Management Journal (105 citations)

In Hackathons, Minimal Structure Beats Imported Process

Teams that tried to import and compress organizational innovation processes all failed. Only teams that maintained temporal ambiguity — minimal coordination baseline, letting structure emerge — produced functioning novel products.

Lifshitz-Assaf, H., Lebovitz, S., & Zalmanson, L. (2020). Minimal and adaptive coordination: how hackathon projects accelerate innovation without killing it. Academy of Management Journal.
Hackathon-Relevant
STRONG EVIDENCE · Journal of Business Venturing Insights

Prepared Entrepreneurs Gain Legitimacy — With or Without Exaggeration

Well-prepared entrepreneurs had high probability of gaining legitimacy regardless of whether they exaggerated in their pitch. But in 20% of cases, investors could not distinguish preparation from misrepresentation.

Cottle, G.W. & Anderson, B.S. (2020). The temptation of exaggeration: exploring the line between preparedness and misrepresentation. Journal of Business Venturing Insights, 14.

5. Hackathon-Specific Science

Team Transactive Memory — Skill-Map at Start

202 Global Game Jam teams (48h): teams that knew who knew what (transactive memory systems) performed better. Task conflict amplified benefits when TMS was present; relationship conflict attenuated them.

O'Toole, J. et al. (2022). Transactive memory systems, temporary teams, and conflict. Journal of Management.
Actionable

Hierarchy Kills Idea Quality

308 teams in #EUvsVirus hackathon: hierarchical cultural values correlated with lower idea quality. Flat teams produced better ideas.

Krebs, B.P. et al. (2025). Revealing the impact of cultural value orientations on creative outcomes. R&D Management.
Actionable

193,353 Hackathon Projects Analyzed

Computational creativity analysis (novelty × usefulness) of 10,363 refined projects. Largest-scale quantitative study of hackathon output to date.

CHI 2025. How hackathons foster creativity? Toward automated assessment of large-scale creativity. Proceedings of the 2025 CHI Conference.
BOTTOM LINE

6. The Honest Pitch — What It Sounds Like

"
We noticed X. It bothered us. We tried three directions — two failed. This one works. Here is the data we gathered in 48 hours. We do not know if it scales — but we think it might, and here is why. We need help.
— The most honest thing you can say on a hackathon stage

Why This Works

No folk psychology. No 初心, no 主体性, no self-branding theater. Just: observation → test → result → uncertainty → ask.

Costly signals replace cheap talk. Working prototype. Test data. Failed attempts admitted. This is what a rational observer should update on.

Survivorship bias is named, not hidden. "We do not know if it scales" is the most disarming sentence in a pitch room. Every judge has heard a hundred people claim certainty. Zero have been right.

Confidence filter is inverted. The most confident people in the room are the least likely to be accurate. Showing uncertainty is not weakness — it is metacognitive accuracy.

On Jobs vs Cook vs Huang: Jobs' abrasiveness was a byproduct of his success, not its cause. The same trait in a failed founder is called "arrogance." Cook and Huang prove you can build trillion-dollar companies without the theater. The behaviors that get mythologized are the ones that happened to coexist with success — not the ones that produced it.
